On-Chain and Off-Chain

On-Chain and Off-Chain

0. Staff-level lens

The central design question in Web3 is what the chain must do and what it can merely check. Everything on-chain is expensive, public, slow, and hard to change; everything off-chain needs a trust or verification story. At Staff and Principal scope you are expected to:

  • draw the line deliberately, with an explicit statement of the trust assumption behind each off-chain component;
  • choose verification patterns (signatures, Merkle proofs, optimistic or validity proofs, commit-reveal) that let the chain enforce rules without doing all the work;
  • keep on-chain and off-chain state consistent through reorgs, failures, and upgrades;
  • price the design (gas, data availability, infrastructure) and plan for what happens when the off-chain part fails or turns hostile.

Companion files: 05_web3-architecture.org, 10_oracles-and-mev.org, 11_layer-2-and-cross-chain.org, 13_indexing-and-data-access.org. Software Architecture notes: boundaries, contracts, and patterns (outbox, saga).

1. What belongs on-chain

Keep on-chain only what needs the chain's unique properties:

  • Ownership and custody of assets and the rules for moving them.
  • Settlement and finality: the irreversible record that something happened.
  • Rules that must be enforced without trusting an operator: access control, accounting, invariants such as solvency.
  • Composability points: interfaces other contracts need to call atomically.
  • Commitments: hashes and roots that bind off-chain data (Merkle roots, content hashes, state roots).
  • Dispute and exit mechanisms: the ability to challenge, force inclusion, or withdraw when off-chain actors fail.

Everything else (computation, matching, ranking, search, storage of large data, notifications, UI, analytics, personal data) should usually live off-chain, with the chain verifying or settling the result.

2. Patterns for combining the two

3. Data availability and storage

  • Data availability (DA): for a system to be verifiable or recoverable, the data needed to reconstruct state must be retrievable by anyone. On Ethereum L1, calldata and blobs provide DA; since EIP-4844, blobs are the cheap option for rollups (they are retained for a limited window, so long-term archival is a separate concern). DA layers outside Ethereum (Celestia, EigenDA, Avail and others) trade security assumptions for cost.
  • Content addressing: IPFS identifies data by its hash (CID); anyone can pin and serve it, but availability requires at least one pinner. Arweave aims at permanent storage with an endowment model; Filecoin uses storage deals. Choose by durability requirements and cost; pin through more than one provider; document the permanence promise.
  • Mutable versus immutable data: a token URI pointing to a mutable web server can change what the token means; an immutable CID cannot. State clearly which parts are mutable and who can change them.
  • Personal and regulated data stays off-chain: write only commitments or pseudonymous identifiers on-chain; deletion rights and retention rules conflict with immutable ledgers.
  • Encrypted data on-chain is public forever: encryption that is safe today may not be later; do not put sensitive data on-chain even encrypted unless the threat model allows it.

4. Keeping both sides consistent

  • The chain is the source of truth for on-chain state; off-chain stores are derived views. Derive them from events and state with reorg-aware, idempotent processing (see 13_indexing-and-data-access.org and 12_distributed-systems.org).
  • Write path: user intents or signed messages go off-chain, a service batches and submits them; the service must handle failures (a transaction not mined, reverted, or reorged) and make retries idempotent.
  • Read path: off-chain reads of chain state have latency and may be stale or inconsistent across providers; for critical decisions, read at the safe or finalized block, or verify against on-chain state before acting.
  • Reconciliation: periodically compare off-chain records (balances, positions, orders) with on-chain truth; alert on drift; define who is authoritative in each case.
  • Ordering and race conditions: off-chain order books and on-chain state can disagree (an order is valid when matched and invalid at settlement); settlement logic must re-check conditions on-chain.
  • Versioning and upgrades: contract upgrades and event schema changes must be coordinated with off-chain consumers (indexers, bots, frontends); version events and expose a registry of current addresses.

5. Designing for failure of the off-chain part

  • Liveness: what happens if the relayer, sequencer, keeper, or oracle stops? Provide fallbacks (anyone can submit, forced inclusion, user-triggered withdrawals) or at least a bounded loss and a clear degraded mode.
  • Censorship: can the off-chain operator refuse specific users? Provide an on-chain path that cannot be blocked, even if slower or costlier.
  • Malice: can the operator steal or misreport? Bound their power with proofs, challenge windows, collateral (bonds) and slashing, and independent verification.
  • Data loss: if the data store disappears, can state be reconstructed? Keep data availability guarantees and backups appropriate to the value.
  • Upgrade or migration: can the off-chain system be replaced without breaking users? Avoid hard-coded dependencies on one operator's endpoints; publish interfaces and addresses on-chain or in registries.
  • Exit: define and test how users leave if the operator is gone.

6. Worked example: an off-chain order book with on-chain settlement

  • Flow: makers sign orders (EIP-712) with price, amount, nonce, and deadline; a matching engine off-chain pairs orders; a relayer submits matched pairs to a settlement contract; the contract verifies signatures, enforces limits and nonces, and swaps assets atomically.
  • On-chain: signature checks, replay protection, fill accounting (partial fills), cancellation (a nonce or order-hash invalidation by the maker), token transfers via approvals, and fees.
  • Off-chain: order storage, matching, price discovery, API, and risk checks.
  • Trust and risks: the operator controls matching and ordering and can front-run or censor; users keep custody and can cancel on-chain; signatures must be scoped and short-lived; approvals to the settlement contract are a standing risk (use exact approvals or permits); a bug in the settlement contract affects all makers; the operator's infrastructure is a liveness dependency.
  • Improvements: batch auctions to remove ordering advantages (see 10_oracles-and-mev.org), multiple independent relayers, on-chain cancellation and expiry, and monitoring that compares fills with orders.

7. Cost and capacity modeling

  • On-chain cost per action: gas times gas price (and L1 data fees on L2s); multiply by expected volume at median and spike prices; include approvals, failures, and retries.
  • Off-chain cost: infrastructure, RPC and indexing, storage, monitoring, and engineering time; weigh against on-chain savings.
  • Amortization: batching, Merkle roots, and rollups reduce per-user cost at the price of complexity and assumptions.
  • Scale limits: the chain's throughput caps on-chain operations; design so that per-user work scales off-chain and only constant or logarithmic work lands on-chain.

8. Self-check

  1. For a protocol you know, list every off-chain component and write one sentence on what it can do wrong and what protects users.
  2. Why must a settlement contract re-check order validity on-chain even if an off-chain engine already checked it?
  3. Compare optimistic and validity-proof verification in trust assumptions, latency, and cost.
  4. How would you design an airdrop for one million recipients, and what are the failure modes of the Merkle approach?
  5. Your NFT metadata sits at a URL owned by the team. What are the issues and the options?
  6. What is the minimum an operator-dependent system must provide to pass a "walkaway test"?

9. Exercises

  • Warm-up: implement a Merkle airdrop contract and a script that builds the tree and proofs; test invalid proofs and double claims.
  • Core: implement signed-order settlement with EIP-712, nonces, expiry, partial fills, and cancellation; fuzz the accounting.
  • Core: build a reconciliation job that compares an indexer's balances with on-chain balanceOf at a fixed block and reports drift.
  • Core: design and test an escape hatch (forced exit) for an operator-dependent system, including the case where the operator is offline.
  • Stretch: implement a commit-reveal auction with deposits and penalties for non-reveal; analyze griefing.
  • Stretch: write the cost model (spreadsheet or script) for a feature at three gas prices and two chains; choose the design that meets the budget.

What to remember

  • Put on-chain what needs trustless enforcement and composability; verify, do not recompute.
  • Every off-chain component carries a trust assumption; document it and bound it with proofs, bonds, or exits.
  • Derived off-chain state must be reorg-aware, idempotent, and reconcilable.
  • Design the failure of the off-chain part first: liveness, censorship, malice, data loss, exit.
  • Model costs on-chain and off-chain at spike conditions.
