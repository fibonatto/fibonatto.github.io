Transactions and Gas

Transactions and Gas

0. Staff-level lens

Transactions are how everything happens on a chain, and gas is both the unit of cost and the unit of denial-of-service defense. Most "mysterious" production problems (stuck transactions, nonce gaps, failed submissions, surprising costs, reorg-induced inconsistencies) are transaction-lifecycle problems. At Staff and Principal scope you are expected to:

  • model the full lifecycle of a transaction and design services that submit, track, and reconcile transactions correctly;
  • understand the fee market and the cost model well enough to estimate and bound costs and to optimize where it matters;
  • set confirmation and retry policies appropriate to the value at stake;
  • explain trade-offs between gas optimization, readability, and security.

Companion files: 01_blockchain-fundamentals.org, 02_evm-and-solidity.org, 12_distributed-systems.org, 17_reliability-and-monitoring.org. In the Foundations notes: queueing and overload (fee dynamics as a congestion price).

1. Anatomy of a transaction

  • Fields: chain id, nonce, gas limit, fee parameters (maxFeePerGas and maxPriorityFeePerGas for current types), to (empty for contract creation), value, data (calldata or init code), an optional access list, and a signature from which the sender is recovered.
  • Types: type 0 (legacy), type 1 (EIP-2930, access lists), type 2 (EIP-1559, the default fee model), type 3 (EIP-4844, blob-carrying, with blob fee fields and blob hashes), and type 4 (EIP-7702, with an authorization list that lets an EOA delegate to contract code).
  • Nonce: a per-account counter. Transactions from one account execute in strict nonce order; a gap blocks all later nonces until filled.
  • Gas limit versus gas used: the limit is a cap you set (unused gas is refunded); the used gas is what execution consumed. A transaction that runs out of gas still pays for the gas used. A per-transaction gas cap exists in recent protocol versions (about 16.8 million gas since the Fusaka upgrade; check the current rules) in addition to the block gas limit.
  • Receipts: status (success or revert), gas used, effective gas price, logs, and for blob transactions the blob fee data. Reverted transactions still consume gas and are included in the block.

2. Lifecycle

  1. Construct and sign locally (see 08_wallets-and-cryptography.org); simulate first (eth_call or a simulator) to catch reverts.
  2. Broadcast to a node (or relay/builder endpoint). The node validates (signature, nonce, balance for max cost, fee floor) and gossips through the mempool.
  3. Pending: waiting in mempools; builders pick transactions by fee and bundle value. Nodes drop transactions after their own limits (size, age, underpriced). The mempool is not a global queue: each node has its own view.
  4. Included in a block (a proposer's block). Execution succeeded or reverted; logs emitted.
  5. Confirmed: subsequent blocks build on it. Each confirmation reduces the chance of a reorg.
  6. Safe and finalized: Ethereum exposes block tags; safe (a justified checkpoint, roughly one epoch) and finalized (two epochs, roughly 13 minutes) carry stronger guarantees than latest.
  7. Replaced or dropped: a transaction with the same nonce and higher fees (clients require a minimum bump, commonly 10 percent) replaces the pending one; otherwise it may linger or be evicted.
  8. Reorged: included transactions may disappear and return to the mempool; their effects vanish with the block.

Design consequences: sending a transaction is at-least-once and unordered from the application's perspective; the only truth is the chain state at a chosen finality level (see 12_distributed-systems.org).

3. The fee market and gas accounting

  • EIP-1559: each block has a base fee per gas, determined by the protocol; every transaction pays at least the base fee (burned) plus a priority fee to the proposer, up to maxFeePerGas in total. The base fee moves toward a target of half the block gas limit: if the previous block was full it rises by up to 12.5 percent, if empty it falls by up to 12.5 percent. Next base fee is approximately the current one times (1 + (used - target) / target / 8).
  • Effective price = min(maxFeePerGas, baseFee + maxPriorityFeePerGas). Setting a high maxFeePerGas is safe because you pay only the effective price; setting a maxPriorityFeePerGas high pays more for faster inclusion.
  • Block gas limit is set by validators' voting within bounds; it has been raised several times since 2025 and continues to be adjusted (check the current value and the protocol's default target). Higher limits raise throughput and node burden.
  • Gas schedule highlights (verify against the current fork; values change through repricing EIPs):

  • Intrinsic gas is charged before execution; if the gas limit is below it, the transaction is invalid. Access lists pre-declare accessed addresses and slots at a fixed discount and are rarely worth the effort now except in specific cases.
  • Refunds are limited and no longer encourage gas tokens.
  • Blobs (EIP-4844): 128 KiB per blob, priced in a separate blob fee market and retained only for a limited period by the network (weeks). Target and maximum blobs per block have been increased through Pectra and the blob-parameter-only forks after Fusaka. Rollups use blobs to post data cheaply (see 11_layer-2-and-cross-chain.org). Contracts cannot read blob contents, only commitments.
  • L2 fees: an L2 transaction pays for L2 execution plus the cost of posting its data to L1 (blobs or calldata). Both components vary; when blob prices are low, L2 fees are dominated by execution and sequencer margin. Fee behavior and APIs differ per L2; read each one's documentation.

4. Estimating, bounding, and managing cost

  • Estimate: eth_estimateGas simulates the call at the current state; add a safety margin because state may change before inclusion; for contracts with data-dependent paths, estimate on the worst-case path.
  • Fee estimation: use the node's fee history or a library estimator to pick a priority fee and a max fee; set maxFeePerGas with headroom (for example two times the base fee) so that the transaction remains valid through a few blocks of base-fee growth.
  • Bound the worst case: compute maximum cost as gasLimit * maxFeePerGas (plus value); that is the balance a sender needs and the most it can spend.
  • Budgeting for services: relayers, keepers, and bots need gas funds, automated top-ups, and alerts for low balance; they need caps on per-action gas price to avoid being drained during spikes.
  • Gas as a security feature: user-controlled loops must be bounded because attackers can make operations exceed the block gas limit; gas griefing (forcing out-of-gas in a callee) must be considered when forwarding gas.
  • Cost modeling: estimate cost per user action at median and at 10x gas prices, on each chain; include deployment, approvals (often two transactions: approve then act), and failed-transaction costs.

5. Optimizing gas without paying for it in security

Gas optimization has real value for high-volume code and little value elsewhere; every optimization is a risk to readability and audit.

  • Measure first: gas reports and snapshots (forge test --gas-report, forge snapshot), tracing opcodes of hot paths; optimize what is called frequently.
  • High-yield techniques: minimize storage writes (the dominant cost); pack variables that are read and written together; cache storage reads in memory; use immutable and constant; prefer calldata for read-only external arrays; use custom errors; batch operations to amortize the base cost and cold accesses; use events instead of storage for data that only off-chain systems need; use bitmaps and Merkle proofs instead of per-user storage (airdrops, allowlists); use minimal proxies for many instances; use transient storage for per-transaction locks and accounting.
  • Lower-yield or risky techniques: inline assembly, unchecked arithmetic without proofs, unusual bit packing, unrolled loops, and hand-written storage layouts. Use only in hot paths, with tests, comments, and review. Gas-optimized libraries trade safety checks for speed; read what they do not check.
  • Compiler settings: the optimizer runs parameter and the IR pipeline affect both size and cost; benchmark with realistic call patterns, not a single function.
  • Know the trade-off: contract size limits (24,576 bytes) constrain inlining; splitting into libraries or modules adds call overhead.
  • Re-measure after repricing forks: gas costs change with protocol upgrades; an optimization that once mattered may disappear, and vice versa.

6. Building a reliable transaction-submitting service

Services that send transactions (relayers, keepers, payment processors, bots, back-office tools) need engineering equal to any other distributed system. A robust design:

  • Persist intents before sending (an outbox): record the intended action with an idempotency key, the account, and the target; a worker turns intents into transactions.
  • Single-writer nonce management per account: one component owns the account's nonce sequence (a queue per account); gaps stall all later transactions. Use multiple sender accounts for parallelism instead of racing on one nonce.
  • Track state machine per transaction: created, signed, broadcast, pending, included, confirmed at N, safe, finalized, failed, replaced, dropped, reorged. Store transaction hashes, nonce, fee parameters, and block data.
  • Replace stuck transactions deliberately: resend with the same nonce and bumped fees (at least the client minimum), or send a zero-value self-transfer with the same nonce to cancel; never let two logical actions share a nonce accidentally.
  • Idempotency: before resending an intent, check chain state (did the effect already happen?) rather than assuming the previous attempt failed; make contract calls idempotent where possible.
  • Confirmation policy by value: define tiers (for example, small amounts accepted after a few confirmations, large amounts after the safe or finalized tag), and communicate provisional states in the product.
  • Reorg handling: if a transaction's block is reorged, move it back to pending; derived state (balances, credits) must be reversible until the chosen finality level.
  • Simulation before send: dry-run with eth_call at the pending or latest state to avoid sending doomed transactions; handle simulations that pass but fail on-chain (state changed).
  • Fee policy: dynamic fees with caps; escalate priority fee over time; emergency mode for urgent actions (liquidations).
  • Multi-provider submission: send through several RPC endpoints or builders to avoid a single point of failure; dedupe by hash.
  • Private submission for MEV-sensitive actions (see 10_oracles-and-mev.org).
  • Observability: metrics for pending age, nonce gaps, replacement rate, failure reasons, fee paid, confirmation latency percentiles; alerts on stuck nonces and low balances (see 17_reliability-and-monitoring.org).
  • Key security: signing keys in KMS, MPC, or HSM with spend policies; separate hot wallets with small balances from treasury.
// Sketch with viem: estimate fees, send with caps, wait for a confirmation level.
import { createPublicClient, createWalletClient, http, parseEther } from 'viem'
import { privateKeyToAccount } from 'viem/accounts'
import { mainnet } from 'viem/chains'

const account = privateKeyToAccount(process.env.SIGNER_KEY as `0x${string}`) // use KMS/HSM in production
const publicClient = createPublicClient({ chain: mainnet, transport: http(process.env.RPC_URL) })
const walletClient = createWalletClient({ account, chain: mainnet, transport: http(process.env.RPC_URL) })

const { maxFeePerGas, maxPriorityFeePerGas } = await publicClient.estimateFeesPerGas()
const hash = await walletClient.sendTransaction({
  to: '0x0000000000000000000000000000000000000001',
  value: parseEther('0.01'),
  maxFeePerGas: (maxFeePerGas! * 12n) / 10n,       // headroom, still bounded by policy
  maxPriorityFeePerGas,
})
const receipt = await publicClient.waitForTransactionReceipt({ hash, confirmations: 2 })
if (receipt.status !== 'success') throw new Error('reverted')
// For high-value flows, check the block against the 'finalized' tag before crediting.

7. Special topics

  • Account abstraction: smart accounts (ERC-4337) submit user operations to a separate mempool via bundlers; an EntryPoint contract validates and executes; paymasters can sponsor gas. EIP-7702 lets an EOA delegate to contract code for batching and sponsorship. Both change the transaction model your backend sees (user operations versus transactions) and the fee flow (see 08_wallets-and-cryptography.org).
  • Batching and multicall: a multicall contract aggregates reads and writes; for writes, atomic batches reduce partial failure risk and cost; check that msg.sender semantics fit.
  • Revert reasons and error decoding: decode custom errors with the ABI; log raw revert data; surface actionable messages.
  • Time-sensitive transactions: deadlines in calldata protect against late inclusion; use short deadlines for swaps and signatures.
  • Mempool privacy: private relays reduce front-running exposure at the cost of trust in the relay and possible slower inclusion (see file 10).
  • Chain differences: finality, fee models, nonce and mempool semantics differ across L2s and alt L1s; do not copy L1 assumptions.

8. Self-check

  1. A transaction has been "pending" for an hour. List the possible causes and how you diagnose each.
  2. Explain how base fee and priority fee interact, and why setting a high maxFeePerGas does not mean paying it.
  3. Why is a nonce gap harmful, and how does a service avoid creating one?
  4. Design the confirmation policy for a deposit-crediting service with three value tiers.
  5. Which three changes reduce the gas cost of an airdrop to 100,000 users, and what do they cost in complexity?
  6. How would you cancel a stuck transaction, and when does that fail?

9. Exercises

  • Warm-up: send transactions on a local node; observe nonce handling, replacement with a higher fee, and a reverted transaction's receipt.
  • Core: implement a nonce-managed sender with persistent intents and a state machine for transaction status, including replacement and reorg handling (simulate reorgs with a local node's snapshot and revert).
  • Core: write a gas report for the vault contract; reduce the cost of a hot path by two techniques; document gas, readability, and risk trade-offs.
  • Core: build a fee estimator that picks priority fees from recent blocks and caps total cost per action.
  • Stretch: implement a Merkle-proof airdrop and compare its gas with a per-user storage mapping.
  • Stretch: build a simulation harness that replays your service against injected failures (dropped transactions, RPC errors, reorgs) and asserts that no intent is lost or duplicated.

What to remember

  • A transaction's lifecycle (pending, included, confirmed, safe, finalized, replaced, reorged) is the real interface; design for at-least-once and reversible-until-finalized.
  • Gas bounds work and defends the network; storage dominates cost, and the schedule changes with forks.
  • Nonce management is a single-writer problem; persist intents and track state per transaction.
  • Optimize only measured hot paths, and weigh each optimization against audit risk.
  • Set confirmation and fee policies by value, and monitor stuck transactions and low balances.
